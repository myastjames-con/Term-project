The three species

Western chorus frog

Pseudacris triseriata (rainette faux-grillon de l’Ouest). Great Lakes / St. Lawrence – Canadian Shield population.

How much has been lost
10%of its former Québec range is still occupied (p. 4)
37%drop in Québec populations over 10 years (COSEWIC 2008, in Environment Canada, 2015, p. 1)
14%of known breeding wetlands in Montérégie destroyed, 2004 to 2009 (p. 4)

In the Boisé de la Commune in La Prairie, one of the last big metapopulations in Canada, 44 of the 99 known breeding wetlands were destroyed between 2004 and 2009. On Île Perrot, 27 of 80 were destroyed in the same years (Environment Canada, 2015, p. 10). Suitable habitat for the La Prairie population fell by 57.3% between 1992 and 2013 (Environment and Climate Change Canada, 2015).

What it looks like and sounds like

A small frog, brown to olive grey, about 2.5 cm long and about 1 g as an adult. It has three dark lines along its back, a wider line on each flank, and a broad line across the eyes (p. 4).

Its call is a long cre-ee-ee-ee-eek, like running a fingernail along the teeth of a comb. It is often mixed up with the trill of the spring peeper, which is much more common. The peeper’s own call is a short peeeep (p. 4).

Both frogs breed in early spring and form huge choruses. The number of frogs in a chorus cannot be counted, so nobody knows how many chorus frogs there really are. Counts are of sites, not of animals (p. 5).

A naming puzzle. Genetic studies suggest these frogs may really be Boreal chorus frogs (Pseudacris maculata). Canada keeps the listing, and defines the population by where it lives (the Great Lakes / St. Lawrence and Canadian Shield region), not by its genes (pp. 2–3).

What it needs, stage by stage

It lives in lowland open areas such as clearings, damp meadows, fallow fields and shrubland, where a small dip in the ground fills with water in spring and usually dries out in summer. The plants are mostly sedges, cattails and reed canary grass, with some willows and alders (p. 6).

StageWhat it needs
BreedingShallow, temporary wetlands, often under 1 ha in Montérégie. Because they dry out, they hold few predators such as fish. If a pond dries too early, tadpoles die before they change into frogs (pp. 6–7).
Feeding and movingOpen land within 250 to 300 m of the breeding wetland. A frog moves only 3.5 m a day on average (42 m at most), so a road or dry field can dry it out (p. 7).
HibernatingSoft soil, under rocks, dead trees or leaves, or in burrows. Almost all hibernate within 100 m of the wetland. They survive freezing (p. 7).
Spreading outMoving to other wetlands, usually up to 750 m, and up to 2.1 km in wetter years. This keeps small groups connected as a metapopulation (p. 8).

Sites where the frog lives have less cropland within 300 m (3%, against 8% where it is absent) and more open uncultivated land (31%, against 13%) (Gagné 2011, in Environment Canada, 2015, p. 7). Wetlands cover only 4.9% of Montérégie (578 km²) (Environment and Climate Change Canada, 2015).

Where it lives in Québec

It used to live across southern Québec, from the Ottawa valley to the Appalachian foothills, west of the Richelieu River. Today it occupies only about 10% of that range (p. 4).

  • Montérégie: just over 800 highly broken-up sites in a band about 20 km wide, between Beauharnois in the south and Contrecoeur in the north.
  • Outaouais: more than 220 sites in a band 10 km wide and 100 km long along the Ottawa River, between Gatineau and Île-du-Grand-Calumet.
  • In 2009 the frog occupied 102 km² of habitat: 60 km² in Montérégie and 42 km² in Outaouais, and the area has kept shrinking (p. 4).

A “site” can be one wetland or several, so site counts cannot be compared between the two regions.

Why it is at risk: six threats

Ranked by level of concern (Environment Canada, 2015, p. 9). Click one to read more.

High Urban development

Frogs live on land that is wanted for housing, shops and industry. Filling in wetlands is probably behind most of the decline (p. 10). Changes to drainage, pollution, and animals that thrive near people (such as raccoons) also reach into nearby habitat, and the loss of connections leaves small groups alone, so they die out more easily.

High More intensive farming

Fields have been filled, drained and levelled, and woods cleared. In Montérégie, natural habitat covered only 33% of the land in 2001 and most frog populations in farmland are surrounded by annual crops. In Outaouais the farming is gentler (more hay and livestock), which helped frogs hang on (pp. 10–11).

Medium to high Climate change

Less snow, faster melt and longer droughts make the ponds dry before tadpoles change into frogs. How big this threat is remains unknown (p. 11).

Medium Pesticides and fertilizers

Pesticides cause deformities in many amphibians, including this one. Neonicotinoids (insecticides) also cut the number of insects the frogs eat, and they have been found in wetlands next to fields. Nitrates from fertilizer can harm eggs and growth (p. 12).

Medium Roads, trails and other linear structures

Roads kill frogs and block their travel. Wetlands cut off by structures were often abandoned after a few years. Puddles in ruts from off-road vehicles can be traps (p. 12).

Medium Habitat succession

Frogs prefer open land. When farmland is abandoned, shrubs and forest take over and can change how long a pond holds water. This seems to have wiped out some local populations, but how big the threat is may depend on the site (p. 13).

What is being done

Already done in Québec (pp. 14–15): a recovery team (1998), a provincial recovery plan (2000), 11 regional conservation plans, a standard monitoring method, a guide for building temporary wetlands, a captive-breeding program at the Montréal Biodôme and Écomuseum (2008 to 2014), outreach and stewardship agreements with landowners, and a 2001 agreement between the provincial government and Hydro-Québec.

Places kept: the Boisé du Tremblay (about 25% of the Montérégie frogs), Bois de Brossard (about 530 ha), Bois de Boucherville (about 188 ha) and Breckenridge (p. 14). Look for Bois-de-Brossard in the “most protected” list on the map.

Goals (p. 13). By 2025: keep the occupied habitat and the number of breeding frogs in each local population, and keep the connections between them. By 2035: make every local population viable by adding habitat, frogs and connections, and, where possible, bring back lost populations or build new habitat.

Critical habitat. The strategy marks 267 areas as critical habitat, 218 in Ontario and 49 in Québec (p. iv). It is partly mapped, and more studies were planned. Critical habitat is the habitat “necessary for the survival or recovery” of a listed species (p. 18).

Can it be recovered? Of four feasibility tests, two are “yes” (frogs still exist, and enough habitat exists) and two are “unknown” (whether the threats can be dealt with, and whether the recovery methods will work) (p. v). The strategy said action plans would be posted by the end of 2020. Check the Species at Risk Public Registry to see what has been published since.

It is naturally rare, and nobody knows how many there are. The most counted in one area was 25, and usually there are fewer. COSEWIC says its populations, habitat quality and number of adults are probably still declining, and the species is “severely fragmented” (Population).

More surveys since 1998 found over 300 new records in Québec, and nine new populations were found in a decade. That shows more searching, not more salamanders (Population).

What it looks like

A big, stout salamander, usually pink or orange with dark, blurry netting, spots or streaks. The belly is lighter, often cream. A pale line runs from each eye to the snout, and the flattened tail works like a fin. Males and females look the same (Description).

Larvae are pale (beige, light pink, grey or lavender), with reddish gills and small eyes. They get brighter colours when they change into adults (Description).

Life cycle and what it needs
StageWhat happens
Mating and eggsMating is in summer or autumn. Females lay eggs every year under large rocks or other cover, in water or partly buried in the bank. They hatch in late summer or early autumn (Biology; Habitat requirements).
LarvaeThey live only in the water, for 3 to 6 years, hidden between stones on the stream bottom (Biology; Habitat requirements).
AdultsTerritorial hunters at night. They eat insects and other small animals, sometimes smaller salamanders. They breed about a year after changing into adults, though later at high elevations (Biology).
MovementThey mostly move upstream. Moving downstream is rare and usually under 10 m. Adults stay within about 2 m of the stream edge. The longest moves reach nearly 500 m over three years (Biology).
WinterThey spend winter on the stream bottom or in the bank, where it will not freeze (Habitat requirements).

Habitat. Cold, well-oxygenated headwater streams in the mountains that flow all year, especially springs, seeps and small side streams without predatory fish. It needs lots of rock or gravel to hide in, and forest to keep the water cool and the soil moist (Habitat requirements). It is usually found above about 100 m (average 329 m) (Distribution).

Its thin, permeable skin and its water-living larvae make it sensitive to drying out and to acid water (Biology).

Where it lives in Québec

Its Canadian range goes from the US border to Kinnear’s Mills. In Québec there are two main areas: the Adirondack Piedmont (Covey Hill) and the Appalachian Mountains, in the south-east, south of the St. Lawrence River (Distribution; Gouvernement du Québec, n.d.-a). A population in Ontario is considered extirpated (gone) and is not on this map.

Canada holds only 0.7% to 8.6% of the species’ range worldwide. The area where it could be found is 17,237 km², but it actually occupies a much smaller area, estimated at 1,416 km² (Distribution).

Why it is at risk: threats

COSEWIC does not rank them in the part of the report I could read. Click one to read more.

Main Changes to water quality and flow

COSEWIC calls these the main threats: acid water, pesticides, herbicides and taking water out of the streams affect the larvae (Threats).

Habitat Development: ski resorts, golf, housing, wind farms

Ski resorts, golf courses, housing and growing recreation areas have caused habitat loss over the past 20 years, and wind farms threaten and damage habitat. These may change how much water the streams carry (Assessment summary; Threats).

Habitat Forestry and clearing land

Logging removes shade, changes the water’s temperature and chemistry, and adds silt. Silt fills the gaps between stones where the salamanders hide and feed, and it lowers oxygen. Clearing forest for farms and towns lowers habitat quality and cuts connections (Threats).

Severe Introduced fish

Introduced predatory fish, in particular brook trout, are a severe threat. They mainly eat larvae, and they do more harm when silt has filled the hiding places (Assessment summary; Threats).

Small and alone. Because many populations are small and cut off, a single bad event, such as a drought or a spill, could wipe one out (Threats). Most of the southern Québec range is on private land (Protection).

What protects it

Law. Québec designated it vulnerable in 2009. It is also protected under Québec’s wildlife law, which forbids collecting, buying, selling or keeping it in captivity. At the time of the report the federal Species at Risk Act listed it as Special Concern; your table shows the current federal status, threatened since 2017 (Protection; Environment and Climate Change Canada, n.d.).

Land. About a quarter of the sites are in three protected areas or are covered by 12 ownership agreements. That is about 127 km², or 25% of the habitat it occupies in Québec (Protection). Protective measures for stream salamanders on public forest land were adopted recently, but most of the range in southern Québec is private (Protection).

On the map. This is the part the map lets you check yourself: use “Only habitat with no protection” and the “most protected” list to see which sites are inside protected areas.

The page I read had no recovery or management actions, so I did not write that part. Check the Species at Risk Public Registry for a recovery strategy.

COSEWIC calls it Endangered because its whole range in this part of Canada is a small area at Covey Hill, and every stream it lives in comes from the same water source. If that source dried up or got polluted, the population could be lost. Growing demand for water has made that risk bigger (Assessment summary).

The status history shows how it got worse: Special Concern (1998), Threatened (2001), then Endangered (2018) (Assessment summary).

Canada’s range is less than 1% of the species’ range worldwide. Covey Hill is at the northern edge, about 12 km from the nearest New York population (Distribution). Only about 2% of the area it occupies is on protected land and the other 98% is private (Protection, as summarized in the report). Please check this figure in the report itself before you rely on it.

What it looks like

A small, slim salamander, 70 to 100 mm long (about 7 to 10 cm). It has 14 grooves along its sides and back legs that are clearly bigger than the front ones. The tail is round and slightly longer than the body (Description).

A light stripe runs from head to tail. It can be yellow, orange, olive, grey, brown or reddish and fades with age. The sides are grey with small white spots. A pale line from the eye to the corner of the jaw is the feature that identifies it (Description).

The larvae are only 13 to 18 mm long. They have gills, a big yolk reserve and a flattened tail that works like a fin (Description).

Don’t mix it up. The Northern dusky salamander has a wavy-edged stripe and a keeled, flattened tail. The two species can hybridize where they meet (Description).

Life cycle and what it needs
StageWhat happens
Eggs8 to 24 eggs are laid in a shallow underground hollow, usually about 0.5 m from water. The soil must stay soaked. The female stays with the eggs until they hatch, in fall and spring (Life cycle).
LarvaeThey live in the water for a few days to several months, depending on moisture, temperature and food (Life cycle).
AdultsThey breathe through their skin, so they dry out easily and hide in damp places in dry weather. They are sit-and-wait hunters. A home range is under 1 m², May to September, and a moved salamander can find its way back from up to 30 m (Biology).
WinterThey hibernate underground, just above the water table, where there is always water (Biology).

Habitat. Small, slow, cold, well-oxygenated streams, springs, seeps and wet rock faces in forest. It is usually missing from large fast streams with fish. On Covey Hill it lives in seepage areas and in narrow, shallow, groundwater-fed streams that dry up part of the year: 80% of the 98 found in 2004 were in intermittent streams. It lives between 87 and 328 m above sea level (Habitat requirements).

It hides under stones, wood, moss and leaves, and burrows into soft, rich soil. Forest shade keeps the water cool and the ground wet (Habitat requirements). Its predators include beetles, crayfish, snakes, small mammals and birds (Biology).

Where it lives in Québec

It was found in Québec in 1988 and confirmed in 1990. It lives in several streams and seeps on the north side of Covey Hill, in the Montérégie region near the New York border, including the municipalities of Franklin and Havelock, on private land (Gouvernement du Québec, n.d.-b; COSEWIC, 2018, Distribution).

Ontario has a second, separate population in two streams in the Niagara Gorge (Carolinian population, Endangered). It is not on this map.

Why it is at risk: threats

COSEWIC ranks the threats to the Covey Hill population from highest to lowest impact. Click one to read more. The details come from the report’s summary pages. I could not read its full threats chapter, so check it before you add more.

1 Dams and water management

Lowering the water table or changing the streams that feed the salamander’s habitat would dry out the wet places it needs. The report calls anything that harms the water supply the most significant kind of threat (Threats).

2 Logging

Cutting trees and urban development change moisture and temperature and add sediment to streams (Threats). Covey Hill’s forest has regrown since farms were abandoned in the mid-1900s, and it has not been heavily logged (Habitat trends).

3 Farm and forestry run-off

Polluted water from farms and forestry, as well as run-off from cities and industry and air pollution, can contaminate the groundwater and streams. This matters because the salamander breathes through its skin (Threats; Biology).

+ Climate change and drought

Long droughts and their combined effect on the water table are listed as a threat. The Covey Hill aquifer has been studied for climate effects (Threats; Preface). The Nature Conservancy of Canada (n.d.-b) also names habitat loss, disturbance and storm run-off.

Other limits: the animals can barely move between streams, need very specific habitat, and the Canadian populations are isolated (Limiting factors). In 2016, several thousand litres of grout from construction spilled into one of the Ontario streams. It shows how fast one accident can hurt a small population (Habitat trends).

What protects it, and what is being done

Law. Québec lists the Appalachian population as threatened (2009). It is listed on Schedule 1 of the federal Species at Risk Act (first as Threatened, in 2009), and COSEWIC re-assessed it as Endangered in 2018 (Protection).

Land. Because 98% of its range is private land, protection depends a lot on landowners. The Covey Hill Natural Laboratory, set up in 2006 on private land, monitors the area’s water with hydrological stations (Habitat trends).

On the map. The map shows where its habitat records are and how much is inside protected areas. Use the “most protected” list to see which sites overlap one.

I could not find a recovery strategy for this exact population in the page I read. The report only lists the strategy for what it calls the Great Lakes / St. Lawrence population (Environment Canada, 2014), which is the old name. Check the Species at Risk Public Registry for the current document.

Four ways to say a species is at risk

The four systems below do not measure the same thing, so one species can have four different labels. Québec looks at Québec, COSEWIC and SARA look at Canada, and the IUCN looks at the whole world.

1. Québec law

Who. The Québec government, through the ministry responsible for wildlife, under the Loi sur les espèces menacées ou vulnérables. An independent advisory committee (three scientists, three people from conservation groups and one from the government) reviews each species first.

How they measure. The committee uses scientific criteria: distribution, biology, abundance, population trends, limiting factors and threats, along with the province’s precariousness rank (Gouvernement du Québec, n.d.-c). The ministry then decides.

The labels. Threatened (menacée): its disappearance is feared. Vulnerable: its survival is precarious. Likely to be designated: a list of candidates, which gives no legal protection yet.

Our species. Western chorus frog: threatened. Allegheny Mountain dusky salamander (Appalachian population): threatened. Northern spring salamander (Adirondack / Appalachian population): vulnerable. Québec’s page does not give the year of each designation (list updated March 31, 2025).

2. COSEWIC (Canada, the science)

Who. The Committee on the Status of Endangered Wildlife in Canada, a group of independent experts.

How they measure. It assesses a species, or a separate population of it, across all of Canada and writes a status report. Its assessment is advice to the federal government. It does not protect the species by itself.

The labels. Endangered, Threatened, Special Concern, Not at Risk (and Extirpated, Extinct, Data Deficient).

Our species. Western chorus frog: threatened, April 2008 (Ontario and Québec population). Allegheny Mountain dusky salamander (Appalachian population): endangered, April 2018 (it was rated threatened in 2007, under an older population name). Northern spring salamander (Adirondack / Appalachian population): threatened, May 2011.

3. SARA Schedule 1 (Canada, the law)

Who. The federal government, under the Species at Risk Act. Schedule 1 is the official list of species with legal status in Canada.

How they measure. It does not run its own assessment. It decides whether to add a species to the list, usually after a COSEWIC assessment, and publishes the decision as an Order in the Canada Gazette. This is why COSEWIC and SARA can be years apart.

The labels. Extirpated, Endangered, Threatened, Special Concern.

Our species. Western chorus frog: threatened. Allegheny Mountain dusky salamander (Appalachian population): endangered since April 2021 (Order SOR/2021-87), three years after COSEWIC’s 2018 assessment. Northern spring salamander (Adirondack / Appalachian population): threatened since the 2017 Order (SOR/2017-112).

4. IUCN Red List (the world)

Who. The International Union for Conservation of Nature, with groups of specialist scientists, such as its Amphibian Specialist Group.

How they measure. It assesses the risk of extinction of the whole species in the world, against set criteria.

The labels. Least Concern, Near Threatened, Vulnerable, Endangered, Critically Endangered, Extinct in the Wild, Extinct (plus Data Deficient and Not Evaluated).

Our species. All three are rated Least Concern. Their populations are rare in Québec, but the species are widespread elsewhere. The Western chorus frog was assessed in 2022. I took the other ratings from Wikipedia, which cites the IUCN, because the IUCN website did not open for me, so check them on iucnredlist.org.

How the percentage is measured

For each recorded habitat, the notebook measures the habitat's area in hectares and the part of it inside a protected area, then divides. Where protected areas overlap each other the area is counted once. Overlaps under 100 square metres are ignored as map slivers.
