The three species

Western chorus frog

Pseudacris triseriata (rainette faux-grillon de l’Ouest). Great Lakes / St. Lawrence – Canadian Shield population.

How much has been lost
10%of its former Québec range is still occupied (p. 4)
37%drop in Québec populations over 10 years (COSEWIC 2008, in Environment Canada, 2015, p. 1)
14%of known breeding wetlands in Montérégie destroyed, 2004 to 2009 (p. 4)
28%of known breeding wetlands in Outaouais destroyed, 2004 to 2009 (p. 4)

In the Boisé de la Commune in La Prairie, one of the last big metapopulations in Canada, 44 of the 99 known breeding wetlands were destroyed between 2004 and 2009. On Île Perrot, 27 of 80 were destroyed in the same years (Environment Canada, 2015, p. 10). Suitable habitat for the La Prairie population fell by 57.3% between 1992 and 2013 (Environment and Climate Change Canada, 2015).

What it looks like and sounds like

A small frog, brown to olive grey, about 2.5 cm long and about 1 g as an adult. It has three dark lines along its back, a wider line on each flank, and a broad line across the eyes (p. 4).

Its call is a long cre-ee-ee-ee-eek, like running a fingernail along the teeth of a comb. It is often mixed up with the trill of the spring peeper, which is much more common. The peeper’s own call is a short peeeep (p. 4).

Both frogs breed in early spring and form huge choruses. The number of frogs in a chorus cannot be counted, so nobody knows how many chorus frogs there really are. Counts are of sites, not of animals (p. 5).

A naming puzzle. Genetic studies suggest these frogs may really be Boreal chorus frogs (Pseudacris maculata). Canada keeps the listing, and defines the population by where it lives (the Great Lakes / St. Lawrence and Canadian Shield region), not by its genes (pp. 2–3).

What it needs, stage by stage

It lives in lowland open areas such as clearings, damp meadows, fallow fields and shrubland, where a small dip in the ground fills with water in spring and usually dries out in summer. The plants are mostly sedges, cattails and reed canary grass, with some willows and alders (p. 6).

StageWhat it needs
BreedingShallow, temporary wetlands, often under 1 ha in Montérégie. Because they dry out, they hold few predators such as fish. If a pond dries too early, tadpoles die before they change into frogs (pp. 6–7).
Feeding and movingOpen land within 250 to 300 m of the breeding wetland. A frog moves only 3.5 m a day on average (42 m at most), so a road or dry field can dry it out (p. 7).
HibernatingSoft soil, under rocks, dead trees or leaves, or in burrows. Almost all hibernate within 100 m of the wetland. They survive freezing (p. 7).
Spreading outMoving to other wetlands, usually up to 750 m, and up to 2.1 km in wetter years. This keeps small groups connected as a metapopulation (p. 8).

Sites where the frog lives have less cropland within 300 m (3%, against 8% where it is absent) and more open uncultivated land (31%, against 13%) (Gagné 2011, in Environment Canada, 2015, p. 7). Wetlands cover only 4.9% of Montérégie (578 km²) (Environment and Climate Change Canada, 2015).

Where it lives in Québec

It used to live across southern Québec, from the Ottawa valley to the Appalachian foothills, west of the Richelieu River. Today it occupies only about 10% of that range (p. 4).

  • Montérégie: just over 800 highly broken-up sites in a band about 20 km wide, between Beauharnois in the south and Contrecoeur in the north.
  • Outaouais: more than 220 sites in a band 10 km wide and 100 km long along the Ottawa River, between Gatineau and Île-du-Grand-Calumet.
  • In 2009 the frog occupied 102 km² of habitat: 60 km² in Montérégie and 42 km² in Outaouais, and the area has kept shrinking (p. 4).

A “site” can be one wetland or several, so site counts cannot be compared between the two regions.

Why it is at risk: six threats

Ranked by level of concern (Environment Canada, 2015, p. 9). Click one to read more.

High Urban development

Frogs live on land that is wanted for housing, shops and industry. Filling in wetlands is probably behind most of the decline (p. 10). Changes to drainage, pollution, and animals that thrive near people (such as raccoons) also reach into nearby habitat, and the loss of connections leaves small groups alone, so they die out more easily.

High More intensive farming

Fields have been filled, drained and levelled, and woods cleared. In Montérégie, natural habitat covered only 33% of the land in 2001 and most frog populations in farmland are surrounded by annual crops. In Outaouais the farming is gentler (more hay and livestock), which helped frogs hang on (pp. 10–11).

Medium to high Climate change

Less snow, faster melt and longer droughts make the ponds dry before tadpoles change into frogs. How big this threat is remains unknown (p. 11).

Medium Pesticides and fertilizers

Pesticides cause deformities in many amphibians, including this one. Neonicotinoids (insecticides) also cut the number of insects the frogs eat, and they have been found in wetlands next to fields. Nitrates from fertilizer can harm eggs and growth (p. 12).

Medium Roads, trails and other linear structures

Roads kill frogs and block their travel. Wetlands cut off by structures were often abandoned after a few years. Puddles in ruts from off-road vehicles can be traps (p. 12).

Medium Habitat succession

Frogs prefer open land. When farmland is abandoned, shrubs and forest take over and can change how long a pond holds water. This seems to have wiped out some local populations, but how big the threat is may depend on the site (p. 13).

What is being done

Already done in Québec (pp. 14–15): a recovery team (1998), a provincial recovery plan (2000), 11 regional conservation plans, a standard monitoring method, a guide for building temporary wetlands, a captive-breeding program at the Montréal Biodôme and Écomuseum (2008 to 2014), outreach and stewardship agreements with landowners, and a 2001 agreement between the provincial government and Hydro-Québec.

Places kept: the Boisé du Tremblay (about 25% of the Montérégie frogs), Bois de Brossard (about 530 ha), Bois de Boucherville (about 188 ha) and Breckenridge (p. 14). Look for Bois-de-Brossard in the “most protected” list on the map.

Goals (p. 13). By 2025: keep the occupied habitat and the number of breeding frogs in each local population, and keep the connections between them. By 2035: make every local population viable by adding habitat, frogs and connections, and, where possible, bring back lost populations or build new habitat.

Critical habitat. The strategy marks 267 areas as critical habitat, 218 in Ontario and 49 in Québec (p. iv). It is partly mapped, and more studies were planned. Critical habitat is the habitat “necessary for the survival or recovery” of a listed species (p. 18).

Can it be recovered? Of four feasibility tests, two are “yes” (frogs still exist, and enough habitat exists) and two are “unknown” (whether the threats can be dealt with, and whether the recovery methods will work) (p. v). The strategy said action plans would be posted by the end of 2020. Check the Species at Risk Public Registry to see what has been published since.

Northern spring salamander

Gyrinophilus porphyriticus (salamandre pourpre). Adirondack / Appalachian population.

Habitat. Permanent or intermittent forest streams in mountainous areas, with clear, cold, well-oxygenated water and a gravel or rocky bottom (Gouvernement du Québec, n.d.-a). It lives in the south-east of Québec, south of the St. Lawrence River, and in the Covey Hill area. It grows up to 20 cm and is the largest of the lungless salamanders (Nature Conservancy of Canada, n.d.-a).

Why it is at risk. COSEWIC (2011) says its habitat is threatened by ski resorts, wind farms and golf courses, that introduced predatory game fish are a severe threat to its larvae and adults, and that forestry affects its streams. It counted 14 locations in Québec.

Status: Québec vulnerable · COSEWIC threatened (2011) · SARA threatened (2017) · IUCN least concern

Allegheny Mountain dusky salamander

Desmognathus ochrophaeus (salamandre sombre des montagnes). Appalachian population.

Habitat. It lives near springs, wet depressions and small, cold, clear mountain streams. In Québec it is found only in a small area around Covey Hill, including the municipalities of Franklin and Havelock, on private land (Gouvernement du Québec, n.d.-b). It is a slim salamander about 7 to 10 cm long (Nature Conservancy of Canada, n.d.-b).

Why it is at risk. COSEWIC (2018) says its whole Canadian range is a small area, all the streams it lives in come from a single water source, and the demand for water is growing. Habitat loss, disturbance and runoff from storms are the other threats named by the Nature Conservancy of Canada.

Status: Québec threatened · COSEWIC endangered (2018) · SARA endangered (2021) · IUCN least concern

Four ways to say a species is at risk

The four systems below do not measure the same thing, so one species can have four different labels. Québec looks at Québec, COSEWIC and SARA look at Canada, and the IUCN looks at the whole world.

1. Québec law

Who. The Québec government, through the ministry responsible for wildlife, under the Loi sur les espèces menacées ou vulnérables. An independent advisory committee (three scientists, three people from conservation groups and one from the government) reviews each species first.

How they measure. The committee uses scientific criteria: distribution, biology, abundance, population trends, limiting factors and threats, along with the province’s precariousness rank (Gouvernement du Québec, n.d.-c). The ministry then decides.

The labels. Threatened (menacée): its disappearance is feared. Vulnerable: its survival is precarious. Likely to be designated: a list of candidates, which gives no legal protection yet.

Our species. Western chorus frog: threatened. Allegheny Mountain dusky salamander (Appalachian population): threatened. Northern spring salamander (Adirondack / Appalachian population): vulnerable. Québec’s page does not give the year of each designation (list updated March 31, 2025).

2. COSEWIC (Canada, the science)

Who. The Committee on the Status of Endangered Wildlife in Canada, a group of independent experts.

How they measure. It assesses a species, or a separate population of it, across all of Canada and writes a status report. Its assessment is advice to the federal government. It does not protect the species by itself.

The labels. Endangered, Threatened, Special Concern, Not at Risk (and Extirpated, Extinct, Data Deficient).

Our species. Western chorus frog: threatened, April 2008 (Ontario and Québec population). Allegheny Mountain dusky salamander (Appalachian population): endangered, April 2018 (it was rated threatened in 2007, under an older population name). Northern spring salamander (Adirondack / Appalachian population): threatened, May 2011.

3. SARA Schedule 1 (Canada, the law)

Who. The federal government, under the Species at Risk Act. Schedule 1 is the official list of species with legal status in Canada.

How they measure. It does not run its own assessment. It decides whether to add a species to the list, usually after a COSEWIC assessment, and publishes the decision as an Order in the Canada Gazette. This is why COSEWIC and SARA can be years apart.

The labels. Extirpated, Endangered, Threatened, Special Concern.

Our species. Western chorus frog: threatened. Allegheny Mountain dusky salamander (Appalachian population): endangered since April 2021 (Order SOR/2021-87), three years after COSEWIC’s 2018 assessment. Northern spring salamander (Adirondack / Appalachian population): threatened since the 2017 Order (SOR/2017-112).

4. IUCN Red List (the world)

Who. The International Union for Conservation of Nature, with groups of specialist scientists, such as its Amphibian Specialist Group.

How they measure. It assesses the risk of extinction of the whole species in the world, against set criteria.

The labels. Least Concern, Near Threatened, Vulnerable, Endangered, Critically Endangered, Extinct in the Wild, Extinct (plus Data Deficient and Not Evaluated).

Our species. All three are rated Least Concern. Their populations are rare in Québec, but the species are widespread elsewhere. The Western chorus frog was assessed in 2022. I took the other ratings from Wikipedia, which cites the IUCN, because the IUCN website did not open for me, so check them on iucnredlist.org.

Conservation sites overlapping

Numbers for each species

All amphibians on the map. Sites = recorded habitat sites. Area protected = the share of all that habitat that lies inside a protected area.

How the percentage is measured

For each recorded habitat, the notebook measures the habitat's area in hectares and the part of it inside a protected area, then divides. Where protected areas overlap each other the area is counted once. Overlaps under 100 square metres are ignored as map slivers.

The habitat shapes are the mapped areas of each recorded occurrence, not a survey of every place the species lives.
